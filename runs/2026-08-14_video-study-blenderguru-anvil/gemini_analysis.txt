Here are the key modeling moments and transferable techniques from the video:

---

### 1. **Reference Image Workspace Configuration**
* **Timestamp:** `02:14` – `02:58`
* **Visible on screen:** The viewport is split into multiple panes. The left is the 3D viewport containing the default cube; the right panes are switched to the *UV/Image Editor*, where reference photographs of real-world anvils are loaded side-by-side (one showing side profile, one showing texturing/wear details).
* **Action & Reasoning:** Rather than guessing or modeling from memory, the artist sets up dedicated image panels directly beside the active viewport. He also notes how variations among reference photos require choosing a specific model to follow for primary proportions while using others for material reference.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** When modeling any real-world object (vehicles, tools, furniture), setting up high-visibility reference viewports directly adjacent to the working perspective prevents proportion drift and topology guesswork.

---

### 2. **Identifying the Primary Geometric Anchor**
* **Timestamp:** `04:06` – `04:35`
* **Visible on screen:** The artist highlights the rectangular footprint at the base in the reference photo using the grease pencil, deletes the default cube, and spawns a flat single 2D plane (`Mesh > Plane`), extruding it upward slightly to form the base lip.
* **Action & Reasoning:** Instead of starting at the complex organic curves of the horn or the waist, he identifies the single unambiguous primitive shape: the flat rectangular base.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** Complex hard-surface and organic models should begin from the simplest planar or symmetrical anchor point where dimensions and orientation are unambiguous, building outward into complexity rather than starting at curved transitions.

---

### 3. **The Trap of Manual Step-Extrusion vs. Continuous Subdivision**
* **Timestamp:** `05:07` – `05:40`
* **Visible on screen:** The artist extrudes a face upward, scales it down, extrudes upward again, scales down again, producing an uneven, stepped pyramid with inconsistent ring distances.
* **Action & Reasoning:** He intentionally demonstrates the intuitive beginner approach to show why it fails: manually stepped extrusions create uneven edge-loop spacing, non-uniform curvature, and imprecise tapering that is difficult to smooth out later.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** When creating tapered or curved transitions (e.g., bottle necks, table legs, vehicle pillars), do not build them step-by-step; extrude the full height as a clean primitive and subdivide it uniformly.

---

### 4. **Pre-Dividing Uniform Geometry for Deformation**
* **Timestamp:** `06:06` – `06:40`
* **Visible on screen:** A single tall rectangular extrusion representing the waist/body of the anvil is cut into exactly 12 evenly spaced horizontal loop cuts using `Ctrl + R` and scrolling the mouse wheel.
* **Action & Reasoning:** Creating equal segment heights along the entire extrusion provides a uniform grid resolution, ensuring that subsequent deformation operations apply smoothly across the whole mesh without pinching or localized density spikes.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** Deforming topology (via modifiers, proportional editing, or lattices) requires even vertex distribution beforehand; uneven edge loops cause deformed surfaces to buckle or crease.

---

### 5. **Constrained Planar Scaling (`Shift + Z`) with Proportional Falloff Profiles**
* **Timestamp:** `06:50` – `08:48`
* **Visible on screen:** An edge loop near the top waist is selected. Proportional Editing (`O`) is enabled with the influence radius displayed as a white circle. The artist scales using `S` followed by `Shift + Z` to constrain deformation strictly to the horizontal X/Y plane, cycling through falloff curves (*Inverse Square* vs. *Linear*, *Random*, *Sphere*).
* **Action & Reasoning:** Standard 3D scaling would compress or expand the vertical distance between loops, squashing the waist height. `Shift + Z` locks the vertical Z-axis, ensuring the cross-sectional silhouette narrows smoothly while maintaining exact layer heights. *Inverse Square* provides a natural concave flare.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** Axis-locking (`Shift + [Axis]`) combined with Proportional Editing allows you to sculpt radial or planar tapers (vases, columns, fuselage sections) without distorting segment heights or edge spacing.

---

### 6. **Hiding Geometry (`H`) to Isolate Proportional Editing Influence**
* **Timestamp:** `11:38` – `12:25`
* **Visible on screen:** After extruding the anvil's horn section horizontally and inserting 5 vertical loop cuts, the artist selects the bottom vertices to curve them upward. Because proportional editing is also dragging down the flat top surface, he selects the top deck vertices and presses `H` to hide them, deforms the bottom edge smoothly using *Sphere* falloff, and unhides with `Alt + H`.
* **Action & Reasoning:** Proportional editing affects all vertices within its radius, even those meant to stay rigid or flat. Hiding geometry temporarily removes it from tool calculation without breaking mesh topology or requiring vertex group masks.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** Whenever global/proportional brushes threaten to warp adjacent hard edges or flat surfaces (such as curved undercarriages of vehicles or furniture joints), hiding the flat surfaces isolates the deformation purely to the targeted flow lines.

---

### 7. **Global Aspect Ratio Tuning against Silhouette Reference**
* **Timestamp:** `12:40` – `13:18`
* **Visible on screen:** The waist vertices are selected with proportional editing, and scaled vertically (`S` > `Z`) down to a scale factor of `0.70`, lowering the entire center of gravity so the model matches the reference photo's squat, heavy base.
* **Action & Reasoning:** Modeling features individually often leads to elongated intermediate sections. The artist steps back to view the global silhouette against the reference, identifying that the waist was too tall and made the anvil look top-heavy.
* **Transferable Takeaway:** Regularly zooming out to evaluate the silhouette and center of gravity against reference prevents "feature creep," where fine details are topologically correct but overall object weight and proportions feel off.
