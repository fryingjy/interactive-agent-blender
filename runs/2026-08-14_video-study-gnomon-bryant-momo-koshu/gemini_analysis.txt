Here are six specific, transferable hard-surface modeling techniques demonstrated in the video:

---

### 1. Projecting Flat Decals onto Compound Curved Meshes (DECALmachine)
* **Timestamp:** 04:00 – 04:30
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  A circular mechanical socket decal is placed on a rounded shoulder armor plate (`G` + `Ctrl` snapping). Because the decal mesh is a flat polygon card, its corners visibly float above the recessed curve while other edges clip inside the surface geometry. The artist opens the pie menu (`D`) and clicks **Project**. The decal card’s geometry instantly subdivides and deforms to shrinkwrap directly across the underlying curved topology, restoring seamless contact.
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Planar decals look flat or clip when placed over complex curvature. Using the **Project** function re-bakes/re-projects the decal geometry to match the surface normal and curvature of the target mesh underneath.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  Whenever adding flat detail alphas or floating decal cards to curved surfaces (e.g., aircraft cockpits, helmets, organic mecha plates), projecting them conforms the boundary geometry to match the surface without manual vertex editing.

### 2. Setting Up 3D Greebles with Gradient Vertex Weights for Surface Snapping
* **Timestamp:** 05:32 – 07:05
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  The artist models a solid 3D bolt fixture. In Edit Mode, he places the object's origin exactly at the flat bottom face (`Shift + S` $\rightarrow$ *Cursor to Selected*, then *Set Origin to 3D Cursor*). He extrudes an outer skirt/flange around the base and bevels it. He creates a Vertex Group named `Group` and assigns descending weights to concentric loop rings: `1.0` at the outermost perimeter ring, `0.8` on the next inner loop, `0.6`, `0.4`, and `0.2` moving inward toward the central bolt.
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Snapping requires the pivot point to sit flush with the base contact point. Setting gradient vertex weights ensures that when the mesh is deformed to match a target surface, only the outer skirt blends into the hull while the functional central bolt remains rigid and undistorted.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  When creating reusable kitbash hardware/greebles intended to sit on curved surfaces, weight-painting or assigning stepped vertex group weights to the perimeter flange allows procedural deformation modifiers to blend the skirt without warping the core mechanical detail.

### 3. Blending 3D Insert Normals Seamlessly Using Shrinkwrap and Data Transfer
* **Timestamp:** 07:15 – 08:50
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  The artist adds a **Shrinkwrap** modifier targeted to the arm armor, restricted to the weighted vertex group. The outer rim snaps to the hull, but shows harsh, dark shading seams at the contact border. He then adds a **Data Transfer** modifier below Shrinkwrap:
  * *Source:* Target armor mesh
  * *Vertex Group:* The weighted vertex group
  * *Face Corner Data:* Enabled $\rightarrow$ *Custom Normals* $\rightarrow$ *Nearest Face Interpolated*  
  Instantly, the specular highlight across the flange matches the underlying plate's continuous curvature, making the bolt look seamlessly welded/molded into the armor.
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Standard 3D kitbash pieces show harsh shading breaks where they meet a surface. Transferring normal vectors from the parent mesh strictly along the weighted outer boundary forces the lighting across the flange to interpolate smoothly into the main surface without destructive booleans or quad-retopology.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  This is the standard game/concept pipeline trick for merging complex, non-boolean geometric details (vents, ports, brackets, mounts) onto curved car bodies, weapon receivers, or hard-surface props without creating pinching or shading artifacts.

### 4. Non-Destructive Panel Seams via 1D Edge Curves, Screw, Solidify & Boolean
* **Timestamp:** 16:05 – 17:35
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  On a car side-sill/door panel, the artist creates a single flat 2D line of vertices (a 1D edge path). He adds a **Screw** modifier with:
  * `Angle: 0°`
  * `Screw: 0.05m` (vertical extrusion depth)
  * `Steps Viewport / Render: 1`  
  He then adds a **Solidify** modifier (adjusting thickness to set gap width) and uses BoolTool (`Ctrl + Numpad -`) to perform a Boolean Difference against the car body. 
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Manually cutting topology loops into a curved surface ruins edge flow and creates pinching under subdivision/smooth shading. By driving a boolean cutter from a simple open edge path with procedural extrusion and thickness modifiers, the panel seam can be reshaped at any time by simply moving a couple of vertices.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  Ideal for automotive shutlines, armor separation gaps, and mechanical hatches where you want the flexibility to redesign the flow and width of panel seams without destructive mesh slicing.

### 5. Adding Procedural Rounded Corners to Boolean Cutters with the Bevel Modifier
* **Timestamp:** 18:25 – 18:50
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  The boolean panel cut in the car body has sharp, artificial $90^\circ$ interior corners. The artist adds a **Bevel** modifier to the cutter mesh and moves it in the modifier stack **above** the Solidify modifier (`Bevel` $\rightarrow$ `Solidify` $\rightarrow$ Boolean). He sets the bevel amount and increases segment count to 2, causing the sharp turns in the cut line to become smoothly radiused curves in real time.
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Placing the bevel before solidify rounds the 2D path vertices before wall thickness is generated. This creates clean, manufactured-looking radiused corners inside panel recesses rather than harsh digital corners.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  Stacking order matters: beveling a 1D/2D curve *before* solidifying gives clean curved corners for sheet-metal panel cuts, electrical channel paths, and sci-fi hull cutouts.

### 6. Diamond Wireframe Conduit Sleeves Along Curved Cables (Cablerator)
* **Timestamp:** 27:10 – 28:50
* **What is visible on screen:** 
  The artist generates a hanging curve cable between two points using Cablerator (`Alt + Shift + C` $\rightarrow$ *Create Cable*). To build a protective braided metal sleeve, he creates a 16-sided cylinder, removes top/bottom caps, and runs:
  * `Face` $\rightarrow$ `Poke Faces`
  * `Face` $\rightarrow$ `Triangulate to Quads` (producing diagonal diamond topology)
  * `Face` $\rightarrow$ `Wireframe` (converting the edges into a diamond lattice cage)  
  He assigns a chrome material, selects the sleeve and the cable curve, and runs `Alt + Shift + C` $\rightarrow$ **Add as Edit Segment**. The diamond mesh instantly arrays and deforms along the curve's trajectory, and he uses `Alt + Shift + C` $\rightarrow$ *Edit Segment* (`D`) to slide its offset along the hose.
* **Artist’s action & reasoning:** 
  Creating complex braided textures or conduit brackets along compound 3D splines by hand is tedious. Combining procedural mesh decimation (Poke $\rightarrow$ Tri to Quad $\rightarrow$ Wireframe) with curve segment deformation allows rapid generation of industrial braided hose couplings and sleeves.
* **Transferable takeaway:** 
  This diamond-lattice technique combined with curve-deform tools works for high-pressure hydraulic lines, engine piping, spacesuit umbilical hoses, and carbon-fiber/braided wire coverings on any mech or vehicle project.
